### 1. Global bullies have turned uncertainty into a weapon {#i-4f650eca16}

The piece argues that both Washington and Beijing see profit in keeping the world on edge—one through tariff threats, shifting rules and wavering security commitments, the other through export controls and supply-chain leverage. Firms and smaller states are left to hedge, pick sides or pay a premium for predictability. Worth reading for refusing a one-sided account, and for asking who ultimately bears the cost of permanent uncertainty.

### 2. Lessons from a miserable month for hedge funds {#i-a20ff58f7f}

A miserable month for hedge funds exposed an awkward truth: outside AI-related assets, little has worked. With capital crowded into a handful of technology giants, diversification and macro bets dragged on returns instead of protecting them. The article draws out the lessons, from crowded-trade risk to faltering hedges, and is worth reading for anyone weighing the AI rally and how portfolios should be built around it.

</article>

### 3. Was Isaac Newton’s contribution to calculus stolen? {#i-e738d8de10}

This article examines Newton’s accusation that Leibniz stole the concept of infinitesimals, separating the famous priority dispute from the mathematics. It explains how both men developed calculus independently and why publication timing, poor communication, and national rivalry fueled the conflict. It is worth reading for a clear case study in how scientific credit is contested and how independent discovery differs from plagiarism.

<p class="deep-source"><a href="https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/was-isaac-newtons-contribution-to-calculus-stolen/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Scientific American</a></p>

</article>

### 4. The U.S. may be on the cusp of losing its measles elimination status {#i-92792e4b1d}

Measles was once declared eliminated in the U.S., but it is now spreading widely again. The article examines whether the country is about to lose its elimination status and what that reversal reveals about vaccination, surveillance, and public health response. It is worth reading for anyone tracking vaccine confidence and the fragility of elimination gains.

<p class="deep-source"><a href="https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-u-s-may-be-on-the-cusp-of-losing-its-measles-elimination-status/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Scientific American</a></p>

### 5. Alien menace or victim of xenophobia? The battle over Britain’s parakeets {#i-48b12ebae7}

Starting with the cacophony of parakeets in a south-east London cemetery, this long read traces how a much-loved Himalayan bird became recast as an ecological threat. It asks whether the very idea of an “invasive species” is part of the problem. The piece is worth reading for its nuanced look at conservation, belonging and xenophobia—and for challenging assumptions about which species are deemed native.
